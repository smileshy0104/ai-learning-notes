## 1. Why are demonstrations of good answers not enough?

PDF pages 57–69. SFT teaches a model to imitate demonstrations; preference data goes further and expresses “under the same prompt, which of two candidates better matches the goal”.

For example, ask: “recommend an activity I can do with my teddy bear.”

- Answer A: “You could read a storybook together, or take a themed photo with it.”
- Answer B: “Stop wasting time on it.”

If the goal is a friendly, helpful answer that respects the request, labellers would probably prefer A. That is not the same as “every preference is about factual truth”; preferences also involve the task, tone and constraints.

## 2. Three labelling formats

| Format | Definition | Example | Difficulty |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pointwise | score a single prompt–answer pair | A=4/5, B=1/5 | different people use different scales |
| Pairwise | compare two answers to the same prompt | A better than B | handling ties, disagreement and criteria |
| Listwise | rank several candidates | A>C>B | ranking cost and consistency |

Pairwise comparison is often easier than writing a perfect answer from scratch, but it is not free of labelling cost. Collecting preferences should not be read as “high-quality data is no longer needed”.

## 3. What does one standard preference pair look like?

```text
x: the prompt
 y_w: the preferred answer (winner)
 y_l: the less preferred answer (loser)
label: under x and the stated criteria, y_w ≻ y_l
```

Comparison should keep the prompt as fixed as possible. Judging answers to two questions of different difficulty mixes prompt differences into the label; the slides sketch Observation=(prompt,response), which is expanded here into the triple training actually uses.

## 4. Where do candidates and labels come from?

<div class="flow"><span class="chip">prompts from the target distribution</span><span class="arrow">→ sample or rewrite with models →</span><span class="chip">several candidates per prompt</span><span class="arrow">→ human or judge comparison →</span><span class="chip">preference pair</span></div>

Candidates can come from different samples of one model, from other models, or from human rewrites. Labels can come from humans, task rules or model judges, but the source should be stated.

Model judges may have length, position and style biases; similarity metrics such as BLEU and ROUGE are not equivalent to general human preference either. Human feedback data also needs a consistent rubric and review.

## 5. A “negative” is not a wrong answer to imitate

If ordinary SFT puts a bad answer in the target field, it teaches the model to imitate it. Preference optimisation puts the **relationship** between winner and loser into the objective — a different form.

That said, SFT’s softmax cross-entropy does push down non-target candidates through normalisation. So “there was no negative signal at all before” is classroom shorthand, not a mathematical fact.

## 6. Preference data cannot fix every error

Slide 63 notes that odd behaviour may come from SFT data quality. Check label errors, distribution mismatch, over-refusal and conflicting goals first.

Evaluation of preference training should include factuality, helpfulness, format constraints and unseen tasks, not only the win rate on the training set. A very polite but factually wrong answer has not completed the task.
